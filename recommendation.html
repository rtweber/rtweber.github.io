Recommendations

Who should you ask for a recommendation?

No matter what you're applying for (REU, internship, grad program, or anything else) the best recommendations will come from people that know you the best. If you're worried that I might not know who you are, you probably want a different recommender.

When choosing a recommender, your goal should be to add extra information that isn't already in your application (or to emphasize the most important information). The people reading your application will already know the grade you got in 311 (you are submitting your transcripts). A letter from an instructor that doesn't know a student well, and so just states how they did in their course has a name: a "did well in class" letter. These letters generally don't help your application.

Students sometimes want letters from an experience that was meaningful for them. Say, related to the course that made you realize you wanted to study CS or go to grad school. Stories like that sometimes make sense in a statement of purpose or in a resume, but they usually don't make sense for a recommendation letter. The instructor can't say why it was so influential for you (only you can!), so unless the instructor can talk about how your skills or experience are relevant to what you're applying for, you probably want to think about other criteria.

Can you ask me for a recommendation?

Maybe! Generally, I write letters as long as I have the bandwidth and you meet at least one of these criteria:

  • I've worked with you closely. Usually, that means you've been a TA for me or we've done a research project together.
  • You performed strongly in a 400-level course I taught. "Strongly" is intentionally vague, because it depends on what you're applying for. You want to emphasize the things that show your strengths and are relevant to what you're doing in the program you're applying for; whether a particular grade is strong depends on you, the program you're applying to, and your goals. If your grade is 3.8 and above, it's likely to be strong enough to form a letter. If it's close to that, we should talk to strategize. If it's not close to that, you probably need to look elsewhere. This item intentionally says 400-level course; Most programs that require recommendation letters will care much more about what you can do in your 400-level coursework than 300-level.
  • You performed strongly in a 300-level course I taught and I know you from other interactions. This might mean you took a 300-level course from me (where you did well) and you were regularly visiting office hours and asking good questions or you answered questions consistently in lectures that I taught. Those extra interactions can add something to what might otherwise be an unhelpful letter.
  • You performed strongly in a 300-level course I taught and you have another very strong letter. Sometimes students need n letters, but only have worked closely with n-1 people, even though they are otherwise a very strong applicant to a program. In that case, you just need another letter to check the box of the minimum number. The strongest letter you can find is still the one you should go for (which might not be from me), but in cases like this, I try to be a little more flexible to make sure people have complete applications.
If you don't meet one of these criteria, but still want a letter from me, you can ask, particularly if you think you're in an unusual situation---it's much better to ask and be sure---but I may need to say no if you don't fit these criteria or have some other compelling reason. I say no to other letters outside these criteria for two reasons: first, I want to make sure I have time to dedicate to the letters I have agreed to write; agreeing to too many letters takes time away from ones that should be written very carefully. Second, when I've written letters in the past to people not in these situations (e.g., I don't know them well, but they got a great grade in 311), the people have not been accepted to the programs they're applying to. Those students likely would have been better served by finding other recommenders or focusing on improving their applications for another cycle.

Plan to waive your right to view recommendations

FERPA (U.S. student privacy law) generally gives you the right to view documents I make about your education. That could theoretically include a recommendation letter. It's important that you waive this right, for a few reasons:

  1. Letters sometimes contain things you shouldn't know. I might make a comparison between you and other students (or even another specific student the readers of an application are likely to know), for example.
  2. The people reading your letter want to know that my letter is honest. If they know you could see it (whether you look or not), they might be concerned I've had to change what I write.
If you're applying to REUs or graduate programs, there will be a checkbox for you to check. If you're applying to other things (e.g., positions in industry) there might not be a checkbox. In that case, I'll ask you to send an extra email that says you're waiving that right.

Logistics

Applying to UW CSE's BS/MS program

If you're applying to CSE's BS/MS program, send me an email asking for a recommendation at least 2 weeks before the submission date of the recommendation (usually that works out to a few weeks before the end of Spring quarter). I'll check-in with you to make sure I'm the right person to write a recommendation; if so, I'll ask for a copy of your materials (a draft of your personal statement, your CV, and unofficial transcripts).

In some cases, I might ask for a quick meeting to talk through your application (to make sure I'm emphasizing the most important points) but usually I can see pretty quickly what I want to write.

I don't need any other logistical information -- CSE makes submitting these recommendations really easy.

Applications for REUs, internships, and other programs

If you think I might be a good recommender for another program, send me an email as early as you can. Ideally at least 3-4 weeks before your first deadline. Tell me what you're applying for (e.g. "REUs" or "data science outreach program"). I'll check-in with you to make sure I'm the right person to write a recommendation; if so, I'll ask for a copy of your materials (a draft of any statements, your CV, and unofficial transcripts) along with bullet points for anything you want me to remember and emphasize (Did you lead a project as a TA? Do an independent project in a course? Figure out a key lemma in a research project?).

Along with the materials, I'll ask you to include a list of where you're applying, the deadline for each program, and a link to each program's submission directions. You should also call out any "different" programs in this list (e.g. are some of these REUs programming-oriented and others theoretical? are you applying to both data science and traditional CS programs?) If possible, please keep the list to 8 program or less. More applications than that will cost you money (in application fees) and take me a surprising amount of extra time (schools often include extra multiple-choice questions with their applications that I have to fill out separately each time in addition to the letter), and probably won't substantially improve your chances of admission. If you want help strategizing where to apply, please ask!

For graduate programs (other than UW's BS/MS program), recommendations are a big endeavor. If we've worked together I'll be thrilled to write one for you, but it's important to think strategically about your whole set of recommendations. You want every letter to contribute something new. Do an independent project in a course? Figure out a key lemma in a research project?).

You should also call out any "different" programs in this list (e.g. are some programs in math departments instead of CS? Are some research-focused and others classwork focused?)
